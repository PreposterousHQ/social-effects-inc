The Lord of the Rings

The Set That Became a Country

New Line Cinema · 2001–2003 / The Hobbit · Warner Bros. · 2012–2014

Our Take

Name the good first: a working sheep farm became one of the most beloved destinations in the Southern Hemisphere, raised from the plywood bones a film crew left in a paddock, because one family decided the leftover scenery was worth keeping. It now employs hundreds and draws visitors from every continent. Hobbiton is the largest residual community asset in modern film history, and it became one almost by accident, so completely that once the family had proven the value, the production came back to buy in, with Peter Jackson taking a fifty-percent stake in the business itself. None of this is a failure. It is a measure of how much was always sitting in the land, and of how much further it might have gone had anyone seen it there at the start, the way Skater Girl did, rather than twenty years later.

When the Lord of the Rings crew finished filming the Shire and moved on, they left behind, on the Alexander family's working sheep and beef farm in Matamata, the bones of Hobbiton. The set had been built of plywood and polystyrene, meant to be temporary. An attempt to clear it was halted by heavy rain, and roughly seventeen bare hobbit-hole facades were left standing in the paddock, with the understanding that the crew would return to finish the job. The film was done. The lease was satisfied. The Alexanders were left with seventeen movie-set remnants and a decision.

They kept them. Russell Alexander, a son of the farmer the location scouts had first approached in 1998, opened tours in 2002 and spent years turning curious foot traffic into a working tourism business, through the family's own initiative. The production that had found the farm by air, sent the New Zealand Army to build a 1.5-kilometer access road, and raised the most beloved set in modern cinema on a sheep paddock had left with no obligation to the family's economic future. The family engineered that future themselves.

Then the value the family had proven drew the production back. When Peter Jackson returned in 2009 to prepare The Hobbit, Hobbiton was rebuilt, this time in stone and timber, meant to last. Construction across 2010 and 2011 produced the forty-four permanent hobbit holes and the Green Dragon Inn that visitors see today. And the deal had changed: Jackson became a fifty-percent partner in the tourism operation. The set that a production had once walked away from was now one the filmmaker co-owned. By 2019, Hobbiton drew 650,000 visitors, and Tourism New Zealand estimated that film-influenced travel was worth around 630 million New Zealand dollars a year to the country.

There is a shadow on the case, and it has been named on the record. Scholars including Misha Kavka, Stephen Turner, and Alice Te Punga Somerville have argued that the Tolkien-tourism wave rebranded the New Zealand landscape as Middle-earth and, in doing so, overwrote the land's own history, including its precolonial Maori history, in the story sold to visitors. The critique is documented in the academic literature. It is not fringe, and it is not the Ledger's place to adjudicate it. The reader who wants to follow the thread can. The Ledger owes the reader the sentence.
