Field of Dreams

The Accidental Asset

Universal Pictures · 1989 · Dir. Phil Alden Robinson · Dyersville, Iowa

Our Take

Begin with the thing itself, because it is genuinely lovely. A studio built a baseball diamond in a cornfield and went home, and two Iowa farm families decided to keep it, mowed it, opened it, and let anyone who needed to walk into the corn do exactly that, free, for thirty years. It became one of the few places in American life where grown men weep in public and no one thinks it strange. The Ledger honors that without reservation, and lingers only on the timing. What the families proved, and others later arrived to build on, a single line in a production plan could have begun on day one, the way Skater Girl did. The benefit was always sitting in the cornfield, waiting to be designed for.

Universal Pictures built a baseball diamond in the middle of a cornfield outside Dyersville, Iowa, in 1988, because Phil Alden Robinson's film needed one. The diamond straddled two farms: the infield and right field on Don Lansing's land, the rest of the outfield on the neighboring Ameskamp property. When filming ended, the production left the field behind. Not as a planned gift, not as a contractual obligation, simply because it was there and the studio had no reason to remove it. The families owned the land. The diamond sat on it. They had to decide what to do.

What they did, over the next three decades, is the entire argument for this entry. They kept it. They opened it to visitors, free, and for years the two families ran competing little attractions a few feet apart, each with its own gift shop, before the land was finally united under one owner in 2007. They managed a slow swell of baseball pilgrims with no production support and no blueprint for turning a leftover set into something lasting. Don Lansing simply left bats and balls and gloves out for whoever came. By the film's twentieth anniversary the site drew around 65,000 people a year, on word of mouth alone.

Then the value the families had proven began drawing everyone else in. The property sold in 2011, and in 2021 a group led by the Hall of Famer Frank Thomas took control and, in April 2022, announced an eighty-million-dollar private plan for youth fields, dormitories, and a hotel. That plan stalled and was scaled back, and in 2024 the site passed to a local nonprofit for twenty-seven million dollars, much of it state money, now pursuing a roughly hundred-and-twenty-million-dollar campaign to build a permanent ballpark and a year-round youth complex. Major League Baseball had already come, playing regular-season games beside the cornfield in 2021 and 2022, with another set for 2026. In May 2026, three Iowa families and businesses pledged ten million dollars and the state of Iowa added three more.

One detail closes the circle better than any figure. The youth fields now rising next to the movie diamond are being lit, in part, by Musco Lighting, the Iowa company that lit the original film set in 1988. The same hands that lit it for the cameras are lighting it for the children. The benefit arrived, real and large and still growing. It simply took thirty-five years and a great many people other than the studio to build what the studio left in a field.
