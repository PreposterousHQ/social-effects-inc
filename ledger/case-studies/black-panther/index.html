Black Panther

Wakanda in the Real World

Marvel Studios / Walt Disney Studios · 2018 · Dir. Ryan Coogler

Our Take

Black Panther gave generously, and in more ways than the headlines caught. What stays with you, once all of it is counted, is a single quiet asymmetry: of everyone who reached toward the communities this film was made for, the studio that earned the most reached, proportionally, the least. That is not a charge of greed. The giving was real, early, and well aimed, which is exactly why the case belongs among the better ones. It is an invitation. A studio sets the ceiling on what a film can leave behind, and this one had room to set it higher.

What Black Panther gave back is usually told as a single number, and the single number undersells it. Begin instead with the range. Days into the film's run, while it was still the number one movie in the country and the cultural conversation was at full volume, The Walt Disney Company announced a million-dollar gift to the Boys and Girls Clubs of America to build twelve STEM Centers of Innovation. The timing was deliberate, made while the film's moment was alive rather than after the receipts were counted, and so was the design.

The twelve cities were not chosen at random. They map onto the communities of the film's most invested audience and the places where investment in young Black students of science was most overdue. One of them is Oakland, which carries the full weight of the film's intent. Oakland is Ryan Coogler's hometown, and Oakland is where T'Challa, in the film's closing scenes, opens an outreach center so that the technology of a fictional nation might lift real children. The donation and the story named the same city, on purpose. Four years later the studio did it again: a second million, for the sequel, to Girls Who Code, the Ghetto Film School, the Hidden Genius Project, and others at home and abroad.

But the studio's gift was only one current in a much wider flood, and not the largest. A young marketer named Frederick Joseph started a campaign to buy theater tickets for children who could not afford them; it spread to more than six hundred fundraisers and raised close to a million dollars, sending over seventy-five thousand kids to see themselves on the screen. The cast presented a quarter-million-dollar college scholarship. Chadwick Boseman, who was privately fighting the cancer that would take his life in 2020, spent time with young cancer patients at St. Jude and quietly added his own money to the ticket campaign. And the productions themselves poured roughly eighty-four million dollars into Georgia in 2018, and three hundred million more for the sequel, in wages and local work.

Set those side by side and a quiet truth appears: the fans who gave five dollars, and the actor who gave his hours while sick, reached deeper into what they had than the company with the most to give did. That is the question the whole Ledger asks, and Black Panther asks it more gently than most, because here the giving was genuine. When a studio decides early how far it will reach, and reaches as far as the people around it already do, a film like this one does not just inspire a generation of young scientists. It funds them at the scale of its own success.

"It is thrilling to see how inspired young audiences were by the spectacular technology in the film, so it's fitting that we show our appreciation by helping advance STEM programs for youth, especially in underserved areas of the country, to give them the knowledge and tools to build the future they want."

Bob Iger, Walt Disney Company, Disney press release, Feb 26, 2018
