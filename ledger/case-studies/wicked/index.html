Wicked

The Ecological Choice

Universal Pictures · 2024 · Dir. Jon M. Chu

Our Take

Start with what is simply good: nine million tulips, grown for a film, went on to raise more than a million pounds for a hospice that cares for the dying. The Ledger says that plainly, and it also notices how little it cost. Doing it for real ran to roughly what rendering it digitally would have. The ecological choice was not the expensive, sacrificial one; it was the equal one, and it still ended in mercy. That is exactly why it is worth looking past such a lovely outcome: the good here arrived by happy accident, downstream of a farm that already gave. Imagine the same instinct aimed on purpose, nine million tulips chosen on day one for the garden of a hospital, a school, a food desert. Same budget, by design. The flowers proved the principle; the next production gets to author it.

For Wicked, production designer Nathan Crowley insisted on real tulips. Not CGI, not silk, not painted backdrops. He told Variety he knew it could work because he had already grown five hundred acres of corn for Christopher Nolan's Interstellar a decade earlier. The principle was the same: real beats manufactured when a team is willing to do the work, and find the right farmer. His reasons, on the record, were craft and vision, not charity. What the choice flowed into afterward was something he could not have planned.

How many tulips became a small accident of its own. Crowley has said he asked for a million. Mark and Susanne Eves, the Norfolk growers he found, told him the number he actually wanted was nine million, and nine million is what arrived: reds, pinks, and whites planted across some twenty-odd acres to become Munchkinland's fields. The image that defines the film was partly a misunderstanding, grown real. And it was not the extravagance it looks like. At roughly four cents a bulb, nine million tulips came to about a quarter of one percent of the hundred-and-forty-five-million-dollar budget, on the order of what rendering them would have cost. The cut heads, once filming was done, were used as decorative roofing on the Munchkin houses.

Then the flowers did something the production never scripted. The Eves family replanted the bulbs and reopened the fields to the public, and the proceeds went to The Norfolk Hospice Tapping House, which provides free end-of-life care across West Norfolk. The openings, 'Tulips for Tapping,' had begun back in 2021, three years before Wicked, and the film's fame poured into them: record crowds, and across its run more than a million pounds for the hospice, roughly two months of everything it costs to keep the place running. A commercial flower farm and a charity it already loved were lifted by a film that simply chose to shoot among real things. None of that was designed. All of it was good. That is precisely why it is worth noticing.

"I knew it could work because I grew 500 acres of corn on Interstellar. I knew I could, with the right farmer."

Nathan Crowley, Production Designer, Wicked, Variety
