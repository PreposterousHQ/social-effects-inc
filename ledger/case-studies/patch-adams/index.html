Patch Adams

The Broken Promise

Universal Pictures · 1998 · Dir. Tom Shadyac

Our Take

The film did real good: its fees built three working structures for the Gesundheit Institute and helped launch a decade of its teaching and outreach. But the man at its center understood it as the thing that would finally build his free hospital, and twenty-eight years and two hundred million dollars later, that hospital is still unbuilt. The story had the subject, the star, and the audience. What it never had was anyone building the hospital into the production the way Skater Girl built its skatepark first. That is the broken promise, and it is the Ledger's clearest proof that a film can be about a thing and still fail to make the thing, when no one designs the giving in.

When Universal Pictures approached Hunter 'Patch' Adams about a film based on his life, he understood it, in his own telling, as the thing that would finally build his hospital. He had been working toward a free care facility in rural West Virginia since 1971, the central purpose of the Gesundheit Institute he founded. The film, from Adams's perspective, was the chance to fund the work of his life.

The film was made, and it was a phenomenon: a two-hundred-and-two-million-dollar worldwide gross, Robin Williams in the title role. What came back to the Institute, per its own published history, were fees enough to build three structures on its land, a farmhouse, a workshop, and a dacha that still host its courses and workshops. Real buildings, real and ongoing programs. Not a hospital. And, by the Institute's account, Universal closed the film with a card stating the hospital had been built, which it had not. Donors who saw it assumed the work was done, and the fundraising grew harder in the years that followed.

Adams has never hidden what the film cost him beyond the money. He told Roger Ebert, who recounted it, 'I hate that movie,' and explained elsewhere that it flattened a life of systemic critique into something smaller: 'I would become a funny doctor. Imagine how shallow that is relative to who I am.' His sharpest words have been about the economics, repeated publicly for years: that Williams was paid a fortune to play him and not a dollar of it reached the free hospital. It is worth being careful here, because the figure Adams cites has never been confirmed by any studio or trade outlet, and because the implication, taken alone, would be deeply unfair to the man who played him.

Robin Williams was, by nearly every account, extraordinarily generous. He supported dozens of causes, gave a decade to St. Jude without a fee, raised tens of millions through Comic Relief, and entertained close to ninety thousand service members for the USO. No record shows a gift to Gesundheit specifically, and Adams's frustration on that narrow point is his to voice. But Adams himself has been just as clear about the larger truth. After Williams died in 2014, he wrote that he was 'enormously grateful for his wonderful performance of my early life, which has allowed the Gesundheit Institute to continue and expand our work.' The quarrel was never with the actor. It was with a process that built a film about a free hospital and never built the hospital. Now in his eighties, Adams is still working. The hospital is still unbuilt.

"Robin Williams made $21 million for four months of pretending to be me, in a very simplistic version, and did not give $10 to my free hospital. Patch Adams, the person, would have, if I had Robin's money, given all $21 million to a free hospital in a country where 80 million cannot get care."

Dr. Hunter "Patch" Adams, in public statements (in print by 2009 and repeated since). The $21M figure is Adams's own and is not independently confirmed.
