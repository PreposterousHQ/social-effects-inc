Schindler's List

Blood Money Into the World

Universal / Amblin · 1993 · Dir. Steven Spielberg

Our Take

Most of the giving in this Ledger is institutional: a studio's gift, a production's surplus, a set left standing. This one was a person's reckoning. A Jewish filmmaker who had been beaten as a child for being Jewish made a film about the murder of his own people, and decided, before a frame was shot, that he could not keep a dollar of what it earned. He called it blood money and meant it literally. What makes the act belong at the top of the Filmanthropic record is not the foundation that followed, remarkable as it is. It is that the giving was settled at the moment of decision, as a condition of the work rather than a reward for it. That is what designed-in looks like when conscience, not strategy, does the designing.

For Steven Spielberg, Schindler's List was never only a film. He has said the making of it changed him, that Jewish life came pouring back into his heart on that set, that he cried through much of the shoot. It was a reckoning, and the refusal of the money was part of the reckoning, not a footnote to it. The profit, he said, had to go back out into the world.

So in 1994 it did. Spielberg put his entire share into a new body, the Righteous Persons Foundation, which by its own count has given more than a hundred and sixty million dollars in the three decades since, from a hundred thousand dollars to a German-Jewish studies center at the University of Sussex in 1996 to a thirty-million-dollar endowment delivered to the USC Shoah Foundation in late 2024. That second institution he founded the same year, and it now holds the largest archive of Holocaust survivor testimony in the world. Two institutions, born of one film and one conscience, in a single year.

For honesty's sake, the Ledger notes that the foundation's recent grantmaking has drawn contested coverage, much of it from opinion and advocacy outlets, with figures that have not been independently audited. That dispute is not this entry's subject, and the reader who wishes to follow it can. What is not in dispute is the founding act, and what it set in motion: a filmmaker who would not profit from his people's destruction, and turned that refusal into thirty years of building.

"It is blood money. Let's call it what it is. I didn't take a single dollar from the profits I received from Schindler's List because I did consider it blood money."

Steven Spielberg, TODAY with Katie Couric (NBC), 2004
