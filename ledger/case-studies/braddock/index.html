Braddock Free Store

The Proof of Demand

Pennsylvania · 2009–2014 · Multiple Productions

Our Take

Braddock is the proof of demand. The productions were willing. The community need was real and right there. By a local station's count, surplus from several films reached the Free Store, each time because one person happened to make the connection at the right moment. What was missing was the thing the Forerunners spent decades building: a coordinating layer that makes the giving reliable and repeatable, so it does not depend on the right person being in the right room on the right day. When the productions stopped coming through town, the donations stopped with them, while the need stayed exactly where it was. That gap, between a generosity that clearly exists and a mechanism to make it dependable, is the whole reason this field has to be built on purpose.

Braddock is a steel town outside Pittsburgh that lost most of its industrial base across the late twentieth century, and in the years when western Pennsylvania's film tax credit drew productions to the region, it often stood in as the backdrop for stories about working-class American decline. Several of those productions, when they wrapped, gave their surplus to a small local operation that handed it straight to people who needed it: the Free Store, founded in 2012 by Gisele Fetterman, then the wife of Braddock's mayor, John Fetterman, and run out of repurposed shipping containers.

The best-documented of those gifts came in May 2014. When the crew of Fathers and Daughters, the Russell Crowe drama, finished shooting around Pittsburgh, they delivered roughly four hundred boxes of children's clothing, along with furniture and an entire set's worth of material, to the Free Store. A volunteer, Shiane Prunty, described keeping the boxes moving as fast as families could empty them. Fetterman's point about it was simple and the opposite of how this usually goes: the production had found a way to turn what a film leaves behind into something useful, rather than letting it be destroyed.

By the local station's count, that was the fifth time a production had given its surplus to the store. The earlier four are not named in the record, which is itself part of the lesson: each donation happened because a particular person, on a particular wrap day, thought to make the call. There was no standing arrangement, no role whose job was to ensure it happened, nothing that would catch the next production automatically. The giving was real every time. It was also, every time, a coincidence of the right person being in the right place.

That is why it did not last. Film production in the region rises and falls with the tax credit that draws it, and when the productions came through town less often, the donations thinned with them. The Free Store itself survived, and operates still as Free Store 15104. The production pipeline into it did not, because nothing had been built to hold it open. This is the exact problem the Forerunners solved in their own corners: Mandelbaum with a contractual rider, the reuse operators with standing departments and warehouses. Braddock proves the demand is there and the generosity is there. What it shows, by their absence, is how much the mechanism matters, and why a field like this one has to be designed, not left to whoever happens to be in the room.

"I think they found another way now to take these items and do something great with them."

Gisele Fetterman, founder, the Free Store, WPXI, May 2014
