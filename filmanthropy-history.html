A Note on
the Word

The History of Filmanthropy, and Where We Stand In It

We did not coin this word. It came to us with a history, and the honest thing is to tell that history plainly, name the people who carried it before us, and be clear about the one part of the field we are here to build.

Where the Word Began

Filmanthropy is a blend of two words, film and philanthropy, and the joining of them was deliberate. As far as the public record shows, the word was introduced around 2007 and 2008 by Ted Leonsis, a media entrepreneur and the founder of the documentary platform SnagFilms, which he built alongside Steve Case, Miles Gilburne, and chief executive Rick Allen. SnagFilms launched in the summer of 2008 with a catalog of documentaries and an invitation to audiences to become a filmanthropist by helping good films find an audience. Leonsis used the word to describe film aimed at the issues the world most needs to face. He built the word himself, and we are glad to say so plainly.

The People Who Carried It

A word only lives if people choose to carry it, and several did, in different places and for different reasons.

In 2009, the FILManthropy Festival began in the Los Angeles area, founded by Jodi Fung through her nonprofit the Sirens Society, honoring filmmakers who use film to make the world better and raising support for the causes those filmmakers championed. Around the same time, in Dallas, the producer Melina McKinnon and her company M3 Films used the word for filmmaking made deliberately to raise awareness and funds for a cause.

The term moved through the documentary world. Pat Mitchell, the first woman to lead PBS and later the head of the Paley Center for Media, used it. Jeff Skoll, the founder of Participant Media, the most influential company ever built around films with a social purpose, engaged with it in the philanthropy press. Filmmakers and critics debated it on festival panels, and journalists carried it into print.

In 2017, Fisayo Fadahunsi founded Filmanthropy CIC in London, a community-media social enterprise that uses participatory filmmaking and workshops to build engaged citizens of all ages. In 2020, the Filmanthropy Foundation was established in Burbank, California, a nonprofit supporting creatives and veterans in the film industry.

Each of these is a separate effort, built by different people, in different places, with its own mission. Each gave this word meaning and momentum long before Social Effects existed. We honor all of them, and we name them here because the history of a word belongs to the people who built it.

One Wide Field

Filmanthropy is where the love of film and the love of humanity meet. That is the whole field, and it is wide. It always has been.

The most familiar branch of it concerns the finished film. Here the film itself is the gift. It moves an audience to act, it raises awareness, it raises money, it changes minds, and the good follows from what the completed work says to the world. A director who turns a film's profits into a foundation is working in this branch. So is a documentary made to support a cause. This is the branch most of the people named above had in mind when they reached for the word, and it is real, valuable, and responsible for an enormous amount of good.

There is another branch. It does not concern what a finished film says to the world. It concerns what the making of a film gives, while it is being made, to the people and communities it touches, and to the wider world. The gift here is produced by the decisions a production makes as the film is made: who it hires, what it builds, where its surplus goes, the time and presence its people offer, and what it deliberately leaves behind.

These two branches are not in competition. They are different moments in the life of a film, the finished work and the making of it, and both can be acts of care. A single film can live in both at once. Together they are the field. Filmanthropy is the name for all of it.

The Branch We Are Built For

Social Effects works in the second branch: the making.

We did not invent that branch either. It has been carried for decades by pioneers, EcoSet, Earth Angel, and others, who proved that a production could treat what it generates as a gift to its community rather than waste to be hauled away. The Ledger section of this site will tell their story in full.

What we are working to define within that branch is one particular practice, and it is the practice this company is built on. We call it Filmanthropy by Design. It is the discipline of planning the giving into a production from the first day of pre-production, before a single frame is shot, rather than leaving it to emerge at wrap or to depend on the conscience of whoever happens to be in the room.

What makes it by Design is not what is given. The form is open. For some productions the gift is surplus rehomed and human presence offered to the community that hosted the shoot. For others it is a share of proceeds, a foundation, or a cause committed to before filming begins. What makes it by Design is that the giving was intended from the outset, planned in and shaped with care, rather than left to chance.

A Spectrum of Rising Intention

Within Filmanthropy by Design, one form the work takes is production giving: what the making of a film shares with the community that hosted it. It runs along a spectrum of three rising levels.

At the floor is sustainable production. It works to do less harm: to reduce waste, to divert materials from landfill, to lighten the footprint of a shoot. This is where most of the industry's effort stops today.

In the middle is contributive production. It goes beyond doing less harm and actively gives back to the communities it touches, in materials, the wardrobe and food and sets and equipment every production generates, and in human presence, the time and skill and access the people on a production uniquely carry.

At the summit is regenerative filmmaking. It creates something permanent that a community keeps long after the cameras leave. Sometimes that is a place. Sometimes it is a lasting capability, a training pipeline or a mentorship structure. Always it is something that was not there before the production arrived. At its far horizon is a production designed from its first day to give the whole of itself, its sets, its props, and more, into the community. As far as our research has found, regenerative filmmaking as a named practice has no prior use. It is the standard we are building toward.

This is the ladder of production giving, and it is one road. A production can also give by design through its proceeds, a foundation, or a cause, planned in just as early and just as deliberately. That kind of giving has been the more traditional home of the word filmanthropy, and it belongs in the field alongside everything described here.

Why We Kept the Word

We considered inventing a new word, and we chose not to.

Filmanthropy already exists, it is good, and it carries the idea efficiently. Inventing a replacement would have hidden our connection to everyone who came before us. By keeping the word, and being clear about the branch we work in and the practice we are defining, we let the lineage stay visible: the same word, a wide field, and our particular place within it.

We are not the owners of this word. No one truly is. We are one more group carrying it, in our own way, and we are proud to do so in the company of everyone named on this page. What Social Effects is working to define is not the word, but a practice of it, Filmanthropy by Design, and to build that practice here in New Jersey.
