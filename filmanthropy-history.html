A Note on the Word

A Social Effects Note on the
History of Filmanthropy

We did not coin this word. Here is where it comes from, who carried it before us, and how the practice we are building both stands on that lineage and departs from it.

We Did Not Coin This Word

Social Effects uses a word with a history, and we believe the honest thing, and the useful thing, is to tell that history clearly and with respect. This page is our account of where the word filmanthropy comes from, who carried it before us, and how the practice we are building both stands on that lineage and departs from it. We did not coin this word. We are working to define a particular practice of it.

Where the Word Began

The word filmanthropy is a blend of film and philanthropy. As far as the public record shows, it was introduced around 2007 and 2008 by Ted Leonsis, a media entrepreneur and the founder of the documentary platform SnagFilms, which he built alongside Steve Case, Miles Gilburne, and chief executive Rick Allen. SnagFilms launched in the summer of 2008 with a catalog of documentaries and an invitation to audiences to become a filmanthropist by helping good films find viewers. Leonsis described filmanthropy as a way to use film to call attention to the issues the world most needs to face. He constructed the word himself, and we are glad to say so plainly.

The People Who Carried It

A word only lives if people choose to carry it, and several did, in different places and for different reasons.

In 2009, the FILManthropy Festival began in the Los Angeles area, founded by Jodi Fung through her nonprofit the Sirens Society, honoring filmmakers who use film to make the world better and raising support for the causes those filmmakers championed. Around the same time, in Dallas, the producer Melina McKinnon and her company M3 Films used the word for filmmaking made deliberately to raise awareness and funds for a cause.

The term moved through the documentary world. Pat Mitchell, the first woman to lead PBS and later the head of the Paley Center for Media, used it. Jeff Skoll, the founder of Participant Media, the most influential company ever built around films with a social purpose, engaged with it in the philanthropy press. Documentary filmmakers and critics debated it at festival panels, and journalists carried it into print.

In 2017, Fisayo Fadahunsi founded Filmanthropy CIC in London, a community-media social enterprise that uses participatory filmmaking and workshops to build engaged citizens of all ages. In 2020, the Filmanthropy Foundation was established in Burbank, California, a nonprofit supporting creatives and veterans in the film industry.

Each of these is a separate effort, built by different people, in different places, with its own mission. Each gave this word meaning and momentum long before Social Effects existed. We honor all of them, and we name them here because the history of a word belongs to the people who built it.

The Meaning They Share

Across all of these uses, the word has carried one core idea: that film and generosity belong together, and that a finished film can move an audience to care, to act, and to give. In that understanding, the film is the charitable instrument. It raises awareness, it raises money, it changes minds, and the good follows from what the finished film says to the world. That idea is real, it is valuable, and it has done genuine good. We are not here to replace it.

What Social Effects Means by the Word

Every use of the word described above treats the finished film as the charitable act. The making of the film is the means; the finished work is the generous thing.

Social Effects was founded on a different premise. We use the word filmanthropy for a whole field of practice concerned not with what a finished film says, but with what the making of a film gives back to the place it is made. In our meaning, the community benefit is produced by the decisions a production makes while the film is being made: who it hires, what it builds, where its surplus goes, and what it deliberately leaves behind for the community that hosted it.

Within that field, the particular practice Social Effects is building is what we call Filmanthropy by Design: giving planned into the production from the first day of pre-production, before a single frame is shot, rather than improvised at wrap or left to chance.

The older meaning asks what a film can say to the world. Our meaning asks how the act of making a film can itself be an act of generosity. The two are not in conflict. They are different moments in the life of a film, and both are worth pursuing.

The Filmanthropy Spectrum

Filmanthropy is not one fixed thing. It runs along a spectrum of rising intention, and a production can sit anywhere on it. It helps to describe that spectrum plainly.

At the floor, sustainable production works to do less harm: to reduce waste, to divert materials from landfill, to lighten the footprint of a shoot. This is where most of the industry's effort stops today.

In the middle, and this is the act the field is named for, filmanthropic production goes beyond doing less harm and actively gives back to the communities it touches. It gives in materials, the wardrobe and food and sets and equipment that every production generates, and it gives in human presence, the time and skill and access that the remarkable people on a production uniquely carry.

At the summit, regenerative filmmaking builds something permanent that a community keeps long after the cameras leave. Sometimes that is a place. Sometimes it is a lasting capability, a training pipeline or a mentorship structure. Always it is something that was not there before the production arrived. As far as our research has found, regenerative filmmaking as a named practice has no prior use. It is the standard we are ultimately building toward.

Three levels, each going further than the last. The discipline that moves a production up them, by design rather than by accident, is what we call Filmanthropy by Design. Together they describe a way of making films that we believe points toward a new paradigm for the industry, and a new role for the places where films are made.

Why We Kept the Word

We considered inventing a new word, and we chose not to, for honest reasons.

The word filmanthropy already exists, it is good, and it compresses the idea efficiently: film and philanthropy, joined. Inventing a replacement would have hidden the connection to everyone who came before us. By keeping the word and being clear about our own meaning of it, we let the comparison stay visible: the same word, a different moment in the process, a different place where the generosity lives.

We are not the owners of this word. No one truly is. We are one more group carrying it, in our own way, and we are proud to do so in the company of everyone named on this page. What Social Effects is working to define is not the word, but a specific practice of it, and to build that practice here in New Jersey.
